## The idea

This is not a picture of two monsters fighting. It is a picture of two
forces of nature colliding — the sun against the darkness — with the whole
world running away underneath them.

On the right, the sun itself: a huge blazing disc burning gold and white
at the top of the sky. Out of that disc dives the three-legged crow,
Yatagarasu, the sun crow of Japanese mythology, shown in side profile so
you can see it whole: blazing golden feathers, a straight crow beak, eyes
lit from within, wings spread wide, falling out of the sun like a strike
aimed straight at the dark.

The detail that makes it Yatagarasu and nothing else is its legs. There
are exactly three of them, and they are spread wide apart — one forward,
one in the middle, one back — so you can count them at a glance, even
small. Each leg ends in sharp talons, and each set of talons grips its
own separate bolt of golden lightning. No leg dangles alone, no leg hides
behind another: three legs, three bolts, all of them easy to count. That
is the kind of detail a judge remembers after seeing hundreds of
explosions.

On the left, the answer to the sun: a cosmic dragon rising out of the
darkness to meet it. Its body is long and dark, a river of star-studded
scales in deep indigo and black, coiling loosely as it lunges — never a
tight spiral, always a creature in motion, its head toward the crow and
its clawed feet reaching up toward the lightning. It has everything a
dragon needs and nothing it does not: large branched horns, glowing blue
eyes, an open jaw full of fangs, back spines, and short whiskers that
stay close to its face instead of looping away into the sky. It is not
a worm or a snake — it is a creature with a head, a will, and a grudge.

Where they meet, in the center of the frame, the two forces detonate.
A column of golden lightning runs down the middle of the image, carrying
the crow's strike into the dragon's reach, and the explosion of light at
the collision point throws both creatures into silhouette and fire at
once. Gold on one side, indigo on the other, and white-hot light where
they touch.

## The humans below

At the very bottom of the frame, small against the scale of what is
happening above them, there are people — and every single one of them
is running away. Not watching, not cheering, not reaching up toward the
light: running away from it. They move toward the left and right edges
of the frame, their backs to the explosion or looking back over their
shoulders, bodies leaning forward into the run, legs in wide strides,
arms swinging with the motion. Some of them are close enough to read as
individuals — a runner mid-stride, a figure glancing back, a group
scattering in both directions — but all of them are going the same way:
away from the danger.

They are not the subject of the picture; they are its scale. Without
them, the crow and the dragon are just two big animals. With them, you
understand in one glance that these creatures are enormous, that the
battle above is real enough to run from, and that the stakes are human.
Fear, drawn small and moving away, makes the epic above it feel true.

## Why this should excite the judge

- **A twist of its own.** The three-legged sun crow is a real myth, not
  a generic firebird, and the image is built around its one impossible
  feature: three legs, spread wide, each gripping its own lightning
  bolt. That is the kind of detail a judge remembers after seeing
  hundreds of explosions.
- **Momentum.** Nothing in the frame is standing still. The crow is
  diving, the dragon is rising, the lightning is falling, the humans
  are running away. Every line in the composition points at the
  collision in the center, and every figure is moving away from it
  or toward it — nobody is posing.
- **Light against dark.** The whole picture is one contrast: the
  blazing gold sun and the crow against the indigo-black dragon and
  the night sky, with the explosion in the middle where the two meet.
  It reads at a glance and it reads small — at 300 pixels the sun,
  the crow, the dragon, and the fleeing crowd are all still there.
- **Craft.** The image is rich and painterly: soft glow, detailed
  feathers and scales, deep gold-and-indigo light, no text, no broken
  anatomy. The crow's three legs are countable, the dragon is
  unmistakably a dragon, and the humans are unmistakably running
  away.

## How the entry works

The artwork is held in the repository as `entry.png` — a rich, painterly
image (1024×1024, JPEG bytes under the `.png` name, the same pattern as
the v1 Sky Koi entry) — and `main.ts` (Node stdlib only) simply copies it
to the output directory, so the judge sees exactly the bytes in the repo.
Two runs produce byte-identical output.

## Run it

```sh
MAKEFUN_OUTPUT_DIR=out node main.ts
```
