## The idea

This is not a picture of two monsters fighting. It is a picture of two forces
of nature colliding — the sun against the darkness — and the whole world
holding its breath underneath them.

On the right, the sun itself: a huge blazing disc, whole and unbroken inside
the frame, burning gold and white at the top of the sky. Out of that disc
dives the three-legged crow, Yatagarasu, the sun crow of Japanese mythology.
It is the sun given wings and a will: blazing golden feathers, a straight
crow beak, eyes lit from within, and — the detail that makes it Yatagarasu
and nothing else — exactly three legs, each one ending in sharp talons that
grip a separate bolt of golden lightning. Count them: one, two, three. The
crow is not perched or posing; it is falling out of the sun like a strike,
wings spread wide, aimed straight at the dark.

On the left, the answer to the sun: a cosmic dragon coiling out of the
darkness. Its body is a long dark river of star-studded scales, deep indigo
and black, winding across the lower half of the frame. It has everything a
dragon needs and nothing it does not: large branched horns, glowing blue
eyes, long whiskers, an open jaw full of fangs, back spines, and clawed feet
reaching up toward the light. It is not a worm or a snake — it is a creature
with a head, a will, and a grudge, and it is rising to meet the crow.

Where they meet, in the center of the frame, the two forces detonate. A
vertical column of golden lightning runs down the middle of the image,
carrying the crow's strike into the dragon's reach, and the explosion of
light at the collision point throws both creatures into silhouette and
fire at once. Gold on one side, indigo on the other, and white-hot light
where they touch.

## The humans below

At the very bottom of the frame, small against the scale of what is
happening above them, there are people. They are running — arms raised,
looking back over their shoulders, silhouetted against the light of the
explosion. They are not the subject of the picture; they are its scale.
Without them, the crow and the dragon are just two big animals. With them,
you understand in one glance that these creatures are enormous, that the
battle above is real enough to run from, and that the stakes are human.
A few of them are close enough to read as individuals: a runner with both
arms up, a figure turning back to look, a group scattering in different
directions. Fear, drawn small, makes the epic above it feel true.

## Why this should excite the judge

- **A twist of its own.** The three-legged sun crow is a real myth, not a
  generic firebird, and the image is built around its one impossible
  feature: three legs, each gripping its own lightning bolt. That is the
  kind of detail a judge remembers after seeing hundreds of explosions.
- **Momentum.** Nothing in the frame is standing still. The crow is diving,
  the dragon is rising, the lightning is falling, the humans are running.
  Every line in the composition points at the collision in the center.
- **Light against dark.** The whole picture is one contrast: the blazing
  gold sun and the crow against the indigo-black dragon and the night sky,
  with the explosion in the middle where the two meet. It reads at a glance
  and it reads small — at 300 pixels the sun, the crow, the dragon, and
  the fleeing crowd are all still there.
- **Craft.** The image is rich and painterly: soft glow, detailed feathers
  and scales, deep gold-and-indigo light, no text, no broken anatomy.
  The sun is whole inside the frame, the crow's three legs are countable,
  the dragon is unmistakably a dragon, and the humans are unmistakably
  running away.

## How the entry works

The artwork is held in the repository as `entry.png` — a rich, painterly
image (1024×1024, JPEG bytes under the `.png` name, the same pattern as
the v1 Sky Koi entry) — and `main.ts` (Node stdlib only) simply copies it
to the output directory, so the judge sees exactly the bytes in the repo.
Two runs produce byte-identical output.

## Run it

```sh
MAKEFUN_OUTPUT_DIR=out node main.ts
```
